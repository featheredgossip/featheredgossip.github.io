How to Identify a Northern Cardinal: Field Marks, Song, and Behavior

By Feathered Gossip · 21 November 2026 · 5 min read

To identify a Northern Cardinal, look for a mid-sized songbird with a prominent crest on its head and a thick, cone-shaped, coral-coloured beak. Males display vibrant crimson plumage with a black mask around the face, while females are primarily reddish-olive with a less defined dark mask.

These birds are common across the eastern United States, southeastern Canada, and parts of Mexico, favouring woodland edges and suburban gardens. They possess a strong territorial nature and can be identified by their loud, clear, and repetitive whistled songs, which are often heard throughout the year as mated pairs maintain their presence in the landscape.

Northern Cardinal Size and Shape

The Northern Cardinal is a mid-sized perching bird, measuring between 21 and 23.5 cm in length with a wingspan ranging from 25 to 31 cm. Its silhouette is unmistakable, defined by a long, rounded tail and a signature crest that can be raised or lowered depending on the bird's mood or level of alertness.

The most vital feature for identification is the cone-shaped beak. This structure is thick, strong, and bright coral-coloured in adults, perfectly adapted for crushing tough seeds. While both sexes have a crest, it is not uncommon for many other songbird species to have crests; the statement implies this is a unique feature compared to "many" others.

  • Total length: 21–23.5 cm.
  • Wingspan: 25–31 cm.
  • Prominent crest on top of the head.
  • Stout, coral-coloured bill built for seed cracking.

Distinguishing Male, Female, and Juvenile Plumage

Northern Cardinals exhibit strong sexual dimorphism, meaning the two sexes look quite different. The adult male is brilliant crimson red across most of his body, featuring a stark black mask that covers his face and extends down to his upper chest. His back and wings may appear slightly darker or duller than his chest.

Females possess a more subtle, camouflaged appearance. They are primarily reddish-olive or fawn-coloured, with brown or grey tones. You will still notice a slight reddish tint on their crest, tail, and wing feathers, and a fainter grey-to-black mask surrounding the beak.

Juveniles are often mistaken for females, as they share similar brown-based plumage until their first autumn molt. However, they lack the vibrant coral-coloured beak of the adults; instead, young birds have a grayish-black bill. As they mature, their feathers shift to show more red-brown tones on the crest and wings.

  • Adult male: Brilliant crimson with a distinct black face mask.
  • Adult female: Reddish-olive with brown/grey tones and a muted mask.
  • Juveniles: Brownish with a grey-black bill, lacking adult red colouration.
  • Annual molt: Juveniles gain adult colours and bright beaks in the fall.

What Does a Northern Cardinal Sound Like?

Cardinals are known for their loud, clear, and whistled songs. Both males and females sing, often repeating a single phrase several times before varying the melody. You might hear them calling out 'cheer, cheer, cheer, what, what, what, what' or 'purdy, purdy, purdy...whoit, whoit, whoit, whoit'.

Because these songs are learned, you may notice regional accents; cardinals in your local area are more likely to share overlapping syllables than those found in distant regions. When they are not singing, they make a distinct metallic chip sound. This is frequently used as an alarm call to warn nestlings and mates of nearby predators.

Behaviour That Gives Them Away

If you want to spot a cardinal, watch for their territorial habits. The male is fiercely protective of his space, often singing from high perches to signal his presence. This territoriality is so intense that males will fight their own reflections in windows or shiny surfaces, mistaking their reflection for an intruding rival.

Mated pairs stay together year-round and often perform bonding rituals like feeding one another seed beak-to-beak. During the winter, look for them fluffing their downy feathers and shivering their breast muscles to stay warm.

Habitat and Range

You will find Northern Cardinals throughout the eastern United States and parts of Canada, including southern Ontario and the Atlantic provinces. Their range stretches south into Mexico and as far west as parts of Arizona and California. They are incredibly adaptable, but they generally prefer environments that offer a mix of dense cover and open spaces.

Ideal spots include woodland edges, thickets, shrublands, and suburban parks. They avoid vast, dense forests, preferring the protective cover of dense shrubs for nesting. If you are looking to attract them to your home, they are regular visitors to feeders offering sunflower or safflower seeds, provided there is nearby brush or trees for them to retreat into.

Life Cycle and Breeding

Northern Cardinals typically nest in dense foliage between 1 and 3 metres off the ground. The female takes the lead in building the nest, using twigs, grasses, and bark to create a sturdy, four-layered cup. A single clutch usually contains three to four eggs, which the female incubates for about 12 to 13 days.

While the female handles incubation, the male remains highly active, providing food to the female and eventually helping care for the chicks once they hatch. The young fledge quickly, leaving the nest roughly 10 to 11 days after hatching. It is common for pairs to raise multiple broods per season, ensuring their population remains stable across their massive North American range.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if a Northern Cardinal is male or female?

Males are vibrant crimson red with a bold black mask, while females are primarily reddish-olive or fawn-coloured with a muted grey mask. Both sexes share the same distinctive crest and coral-coloured beak.

Do Northern Cardinals migrate in the winter?

No, Northern Cardinals are non-migratory and stay together as a pair in their territory year-round. While they do fluff their feathers and shiver to generate heat, the ability to lower body temperature as a primary survival strategy for this species is not supported by standard biological consensus for songbirds.

What do Northern Cardinals eat at feeders?

They are primarily seed-eaters and are particularly attracted to black-oil sunflower seeds and safflower seeds. They also forage on the ground for fallen seeds, grains, and fruits.
