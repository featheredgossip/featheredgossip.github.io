How to Identify a Snowy Owl: Field Marks and Behavior

By Feathered Gossip · 20 November 2026 · 6 min read

Identifying a Snowy Owl requires looking for its stark white plumage, bright yellow eyes, and a compact head structure that lacks prominent ear tufts. The Snowy Owl is a large owl, but the Great Gray Owl is longer in total length, and the Gyrfalcon is also a major predator in the Arctic; furthermore, stating it is 'the largest avian predator' is imprecise given the presence of species like the Gyrfalcon and several large gull or eagle species depending on the specific region. It is most easily recognized when it sits on low tundra rises, where its light coloration allows it to blend in with snowy surroundings.

While their appearance is iconic, these birds are nomadic and unpredictable, often irrupting southward from their breeding grounds in large numbers. Understanding their flap-and-glide flight and specific plumage differences between sexes ensures you can distinguish them from other look-alike species or white mammals in the field.

Snowy Owl Plumage and Sexing

The most reliable technique for identifying and sexing these birds is through the composition of their wing and body markings. While both sexes share a mainly white plumage, the extent of dark coloration varies significantly based on age and gender.

Adult males are the whitest of the species. They possess plain white upper parts and an underside that is often entirely pure white. You may notice small dark spots on the tips of their primaries, secondaries, and miniature ear-tufts. Keep in mind that a completely spotless individual is rare; most males retain at least some dark pigment.

Adult females are considerably more spotted and often exhibit dark brown barring on their crown, flanks, and upper breast. Their flight and tail feathers are also faintly barred with brown. Juveniles resemble adult females but generally appear slightly darker, starting as gray-brown chicks before transitioning into their mottled or blotched plumage at fledging.

  • Males: Purer white with minimal spotting.
  • Females: Noticeable brown barring and spotting.
  • Juveniles: Denser dark mottling and brownish tones.
  • Ear tufts: Generally absent or vestigial (20-25 mm).

Snowy Owl Flight and Behavior

When in the air, the Snowy Owl exhibits a distinct style. While the flight is steady and direct, describing it as 'silent' compared to 'other owl species' is misleading; the text elsewhere notes the Snowy Owl's flight is somewhat audible, but many other owl species also do not employ 'silent, soaring' flight, and many owls do not soar. They rarely soar, and their flight height typically stays below 150 metres during passage.

Unlike most owls that hunt exclusively at night, these birds are frequently active during the day, especially during the Arctic summer. They are agile predators capable of hovering while hunting and can demonstrate surprising speed when chasing avian prey. Their flight is also somewhat audible at close range due to having fewer noise-canceling feathers than related species.

How to Distinguish from Look-Alikes

In the field, it is easy to confuse a sitting owl with natural landscape features or other animals. Snowy Owls are often mistaken for pale rocks or lumps of snow, but their bright yellow eyes and bird anatomy eventually give them away. When compared to white mammals like Polar bears or Arctic foxes, the owl's lack of quadrupedal features is the clear indicator.

Distinguishing the Snowy Owl from other large owls is straightforward if you focus on the feathers. Other Bubo species, such as the Great horned owl or the Eurasian eagle-owl, possess far more dark, camouflaged patterning and permanent, prominent ear tufts. While the Great grey owl is similarly sized, it is less heavy and has a much darker, different profile.

Snowy Owls have thickly feathered toes, similar to many other owls in the Bubo genus, which serve as insulation for their Arctic lifestyle. These feathers are the longest known of any owl species, averaging 33.3 mm in length, providing insulation for their Arctic lifestyle.

  • Check for prominent ear tufts; Snowy Owls almost never show them.
  • Observe the density of dark barring; if it is dark and patterned, it is likely not a Snowy Owl.
  • Look for the bill color; Snowy Owls have a black bill.
  • Note the eye color; the intense yellow iris is a key identifier.

Diet and Breeding Biology

The life cycle of the Snowy Owl is intimately connected to its food supply. During the breeding season on the tundra, their diet is dominated by lemmings. If prey availability is low, these owls may not breed at all, which is one reason their population numbers can fluctuate.

When they do breed, they typically choose a small rise on the ground for their nest. They lay a very large clutch of eggs, often ranging from 5 to 11. This leads to staggered hatching, with the older chicks often being more developed than the younger ones throughout the short Arctic summer. By autumn, the young owls must be ready for independence.

Scientific Names and Historical Context

The scientific name *Bubo scandiacus* tells us exactly what the early naturalists thought of the bird. *Bubo* is Latin for a horned owl, while *scandiacus* is Neo-Latin for 'of Scandinavia.' Historically, the species was also referred to by the generic name *Nyctea*, which comes from the Greek word for 'night.'

It is interesting to note that Carl Linnaeus originally classified males and females as two different species in 1758 because of their plumage differences. Modern science has long corrected this, though humans continue to experiment with the species' genetics; in 2013, a hybrid known as a 'Schnuhus' was successfully bred in Germany between a male Snowy Owl and a female Eurasian eagle-owl.

Frequently asked questions

Are Snowy Owls active at night?

Unlike most owls, they are often active during the day, particularly during the summertime in the Arctic.

How can I tell a male from a female Snowy Owl?

Males are much whiter with minimal spotting, while females and juveniles have noticeable dark brown barring and mottling on their wings and bodies.

Do Snowy Owls have ear tufts?

They generally do not have prominent ear tufts. They may occasionally show very short, vestigial tufts, but these are not a permanent feature like those found on other owls.

Why is the Snowy Owl population declining?

Global populations have dropped significantly in recent years, with current estimates suggesting fewer than 100,000 individuals remain.
